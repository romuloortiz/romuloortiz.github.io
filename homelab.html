01

The machine

Nothing exotic, and not a fresh purchase either — the core of this box is a mini PC that's been running since 2020, given a second job instead of a trip to e-waste. It runs 24/7 in a closet, which is really the whole trick: the value of a home lab is uptime, not horsepower, and this six-year-old hardware still has plenty of the former left in it — bragging rights about teraflops can wait for a machine that isn't older than some of the subscriptions it just replaced.

Second life, not a purpose-bought server

The mini PC and its SSD go back to 2020, bought as an everyday Windows 10 desktop — nothing to do with this project at the time. When its CPU generation turned out to fall just short of Windows 11's hardware requirements, it was headed for e-waste for a licensing reason, not a broken one: nothing about the machine had actually failed. Wiped, put on Ubuntu, and repointed at a job it's genuinely well-suited for — sitting in a closet running lightweight services 24/7 — it got years of useful life back instead of a landfill. Everything else in the build below (RAM, the RAID enclosure, the data drive) was bought new, specifically for this.

Three separate drives, three separate jobs: the OS lives on its own encrypted disk so a stolen or discarded drive doesn't leak anything; the RAID1 volume holds the data that actually matters and survives a single-disk failure; the USB drive never holds anything except backups, so a bad rm -rf on the main box can't take the backup with it.

RAM, the RAID enclosure, and both mirror-pair data drives were the real new spend, bought specifically to turn a retired desktop into a server. The 250GB backup drive and the router it sits behind weren't bought at all, and neither were the monitor, keyboard, and mouse now bossing the box around — all four items scavenged from a previous desktop that went kaput years ago (the same one the mini PC had originally been drafted in to replace), rescued from a closet instead of a landfill. The drive alone had been sitting there collecting dust for years, which is most of how a real 3‑2‑1 backup setup ended up costing nothing extra on top of the numbers above. Tax included where the receipt broke it out; call it a rounding error either way.

02

Ubuntu, and why it's boring on purpose

Running Ubuntu 26.04.1 LTS ("Resolute Raccoon"). LTS specifically — a five-year support window means this box can sit unattended for years without an OS-level end-of-life forcing a rebuild, which matters a lot more on a machine nobody looks at daily than it does on a laptop.

The other reason is unglamorous but real: when something breaks at 11pm, Ubuntu has the largest pile of Stack Overflow answers of any Linux distro. For a one-person ops team — most of whom is, by their own admission, not actually a sysadmin — that's worth more than any technical edge a more "correct" distro might offer. Arch users, look away now.

03

Networking: Tailscale instead of port-forwarding

The box joined a personal Tailscale network — a mesh VPN built on WireGuard. Every device that needs to reach the server (laptop, phone, tablet) gets its own private key and its own 100.x.x.x address on the tailnet; the devices talk to each other directly over an encrypted tunnel, brokered by Tailscale's coordination servers but never routed through them.

Security consideration

Zero inbound ports are opened on the home router. There is nothing for a port scanner to find, because there is nothing listening on the public internet at all — every service (SSH, Nextcloud, the dashboards) is reachable only from devices already authenticated into the tailnet. Somewhere out there a bot is dutifully knocking on every port of this IP address and getting nothing but silence for its trouble. That's a meaningfully smaller attack surface than the traditional homelab approach of forwarding ports through the router and locking things down with a firewall and fail2ban after the fact — this way there's simply no door to pick the lock on.

It also scales for free: Tailscale's Personal plan covers the account holder plus 5 more people (6 total) with no cap on devices per person. Right now the tailnet already has 3 logins and 8 devices on it — laptops, phones, a tablet — at zero cost, which still leaves headroom, but not infinite headroom: every new invite is a deliberate call against that ceiling, not a default yes.

04

Remote access via SSH

With the tailnet in place, openssh-server went on next — this is what actually lets the box get administered from anywhere, instead of requiring a keyboard plugged into it. The daemon itself binds normally; what makes it safe is that nothing upstream ever forwards a connection to it from the open internet, so in practice it's reachable only over the tailnet's private address space, from devices that are already trusted.

05

Nextcloud: the subscription killer

Nextcloud runs in Docker, with its file storage pointed straight at the RAID1 mount — so every photo and document rides on mirrored disks from day one, not as an afterthought. Three accounts are set up: both parents and a 14-year-old son. Two of the three have actually moved their data in so far; the third has an account waiting and no particular urgency to fill it — migrating "in the fullness of time" is a perfectly acceptable pace for a project nobody's charging late fees on, unlike certain other subscription services that shall remain named in the next sentence.

Photo libraries have been migrated in from iCloud, Google Photos, and — as of the last one to fall — Dropbox. One self-hosted app has now fully replaced three separate subscriptions — Google One (the one plan covering both Google Photos and Google Drive — they're not two subscriptions, just two faces of the same one), iCloud storage, and Dropbox — for the cost of the electricity to run the box.

06

Firefly III: the Quicken killer

The newest addition, and the reason the subscription count in the header didn't stop at three: Firefly III, a self-hosted, double-entry personal finance manager, replacing a Quicken subscription the same way Nextcloud replaced the cloud-storage stack — one self-hosted app, zero recurring cost, and one fewer company that gets to email me about a price increase.

Three containers, same shape as Nextcloud plus one extra
appThe web app itself — same db+app split Nextcloud uses
dbMariaDB, pinned to the same tag Nextcloud's DB already uses, so only one image tag needs tracking instead of two
cronA tiny container whose only job is pinging Firefly's own cron endpoint once a day, for recurring transactions and scheduled rules — Nextcloud doesn't need this piece, Firefly ships it as part of its own recommended setup

Uploaded attachments and the database both live on the RAID mount, not a Docker-managed volume — the same "data lives where it can actually be backed up" rule Nextcloud follows — which is also why Firefly slotted straight into the existing nightly backup run (see Backup strategy, next) instead of needing a system of its own.

Environment quirk — sudo has no terminal here

Creating the RAID-side data directories needed sudo, and it turned out neither an automated command nor even the human typing it directly into the same terminal session could get sudo a real controlling TTY to authenticate against — a hard limitation of this particular setup, not a permissions mistake. The fix was mundane: open a genuinely separate terminal, outside that session entirely, and run it there. Worth remembering before assuming any future sudo step will just work the same way everything else here does.

A health check that actually checks something

Firefly ships a dedicated GET /health route that runs a real database query (not just "is the process alive") and returns plain-text OK. Plain text, notably — not JSON like the other services' health checks, which matters for wiring it into Uptime Kuma: it needs an HTTP(s) - Keyword monitor (keyword OK), not a JSON Query monitor, or the check would fail to parse a response that was never JSON in the first place. Docker Container monitors cover all three containers the same way Nextcloud's two do.

First boot took several minutes longer than Nextcloud's did — not a hang, just Firefly running its entire historical migration-and-upgrade chain in sequence on what was, seconds earlier, an empty database. Steady progress in the logs, not silence, is what that looks like from the outside.

07

Alerting: three containers, three different questions

A server nobody's watching is a server that fails silently, usually at the worst possible moment, usually right when you're bragging about it to someone. Three small containers each answer a different question, and all of them funnel into the same place:

Monitoring containers
BeszelIs the hardware healthy? — CPU, RAM, disk, temperature, per‑container stats
Uptime KumaAre the apps actually working? — 6 container checks, 3 real HTTP health‑check queries, 1 outbound ping to tell "server's down" apart from "internet's down"
ntfyThe delivery pipe — the one channel every alert, from either tool (or a cron job), gets pushed through
Beszel＋Uptime Kuma＋cron →ntfy (self‑hosted) → over Tailscale →iPhone

ntfy itself is self-hosted with no public address, so the phone subscribes to it over the tailnet's private hostname. The one subtlety: getting real background push on iOS required routing through the public ntfy.sh purely as a relay to wake Apple's push service — a self-hosted server has no way to talk to APNs directly, so ntfy.sh does that one hop on its behalf while every actual message still originates and stays on the home server.

Cron-driven, on purpose split two ways

An 8am job bundles six separate staleness checks — OS packages and a pending-reboot flag, plus four more added later (see Staying current, below) — and stays silent if there's nothing to report, so a clear box doesn't turn into a daily "all good!" ping nobody reads. A 3am backup job does the opposite: it sends exactly one notification every single morning, success or failure, because for a backup, silence is ambiguous rather than reassuring — you want to know it ran, not just assume it did.

08

Backup strategy

Nightly at 3am, a script backs up Nextcloud's files and database, then (added once Firefly III joined the box) Firefly's uploaded attachments and its own database, each kept separate on purpose, to the dedicated 250GB drive using restic:

Nightly backup sequence
1mysqldump both apps' databases to fixed filenames
2restic backup the Nextcloud data on the RAID (tagged separately)
3restic backup the Nextcloud database dump (tagged separately)
4restic backup Firefly's uploaded attachments on the RAID (tagged separately)
5restic backup the Firefly database dump (tagged separately)
6restic forget --prune — keep 7 daily / 4 weekly / 6 monthly, across all four categories
7One ntfy notification with all four sizes, success or fail

Splitting each app's data and database into their own restic calls means the morning notification can report a size for each independently — useful for noticing, at a glance, if a database dump quietly went to zero bytes while its file backup looked fine. Restic deduplicates block-level, so even though the notification always shows the full current size of each source, the actual bytes written to the backup drive each night are just what changed. One script, one schedule, one consolidated notification — adding Firefly meant extending the existing run rather than standing up a second parallel backup system.

This replaced a setup that was, honestly, just RAID1 — which only protects against a single disk dying, not against a fat-fingered delete or a ransomware scenario, both of which this restic setup now actually covers via retained snapshots. "I have RAID1" is not a backup strategy, it's a very expensive way to still lose everything with extra steps and a false sense of security. A true offsite third copy — pushed to a remote machine over Tailscale, for a textbook 3‑2‑1 — was considered and deliberately shelved rather than left as an oversight: the remaining gap is a scenario like a house fire taking out the RAID and the backup drive together, and at that point the missing hard drive is not going to be the priority. Local RAID1 plus a dedicated backup drive is judged sufficient for the actual cost-vs-risk tradeoff here.

09

Staying current: six update systems, one push

A server that's up doesn't mean a server that's current — it just means it's up, quietly getting older and more vulnerable while looking perfectly fine from the outside. OS packages, two hand-installed CLI tools, eight Docker images, and Nextcloud's own app store all drift out of date on six completely independent clocks — so the 8am job was widened until none of them could go stale unnoticed.

What the 8am job checks
OS packagesapt list --upgradable — names each package and its version transition, not just a count
Pending reboot/var/run/reboot-required present
CLI toolnpm outdated -g for gemini-cli — not apt-managed, needs its own check
Claude Code itselfinstalled version vs. npm view @anthropic-ai/claude-code version — Anthropic's native installer isn't apt- or npm-managed either, but the npm registry's published version tracks it 1:1
8 container imageslocal vs. registry digest — catches a rebuilt :latest even when the tag string never changes; now covers Firefly III's app + DB too
Nextcloud appsocc update:check inside the container — apps version through the app store, not the image
Why a :latest tag lies

A tag like :latest is a pointer, not a version number — the maintainer can rebuild and repoint it any day without the string changing at all. Comparing tags would miss that entirely, so the check instead diffs each image's actual content digest, before and after an authoritative docker pull, against what's running locally. Different digest, same tag, still an update.

Debugging story — when the check itself was lying

First real run flagged all 6 watched images as outdated. Applying them one by one told a different story: only 2 actually were. The original check peeked at the registry digest via docker manifest inspect, specifically to avoid downloading anything just to check — but that endpoint served a stale digest for 4 of the 6 images, a known caching quirk on Docker Hub's manifest-list responses that a plain content pull doesn't hit, since it resolves through a different, authoritative path. A monitoring check that cries wolf is worse than no check at all — a few missed alerts erode trust faster than the alerts themselves are worth — so the fix was to stop peeking and just pull for real, diffing the digest before and after. It costs a little bandwidth on nights something's actually new; every other night it's already a no-op, since the layers are already local.

One image doesn't fit either pattern cleanly: the Nextcloud app container. Watching that story unfold is why a plain docker pull was never going to be enough for it.

Debugging story — the HEIC rabbit hole

1,553 HEIC photos from a fresh iCloud import never appeared in the photo-timeline app's feed, with zero errors anywhere. Root cause: Nextcloud only enables a narrow default set of preview handlers, and the two that matter here — HEIC images and video thumbnails — aren't in it. The timeline app checks a file's format against that same enabled list before it will even index it, so every HEIC silently got skipped before its metadata was ever read. Tracing it took planting temporary log lines inside the app's own indexing code, because every layer looked fine in isolation — the image library decoded HEIC standalone without issue, it was only the combination that failed. The config fix was one line; video thumbnails needed something the base Nextcloud image doesn't ship at all: an actual ffmpeg binary. Installing it by hand worked until the next container restart wiped it out — so it got baked into a small custom image instead, layered on top of the official one, that survives recreation.

Detection and remediation still stay deliberately separate, just with one nuance: the check itself now pulls new layers when it finds a real update (that's what the fix above requires), but it never recreates a running container on its own. Each watched system gets its own one-shot runbook for that half — invoked by hand, never triggered automatically, and each one verifies itself before calling the job done rather than just trusting the pull succeeded:

10

Physical maintenance: shutting the box down safely

Every update runbook above assumes the machine stays powered on. A RAM upgrade doesn't — at some point you have to actually crack the case open, which means the box has to come down for real, and "unplug it and hope" is not a runbook. That's a different problem: nine containers across three stacks, a RAID enclosure and a backup drive that both need to come back mounted, and a nightly backup job that isn't even a container. Two runbooks now bracket that process, the same "one-shot, human-invoked, self-verifying" shape as the update skills:

/pre-shutdown-sequencebefore the power comes off▸
  1. Check for a live backup — the nightly restic run is a host cron job, not a container, so docker ps and docker compose down are both blind to it. Shutting down mid-backup is a real way to get a half-written repo.
  2. Confirm both external mounts (RAID data volume, USB backup drive) are actually present before touching anything.
  3. Pause the 7 Uptime Kuma monitors watching Nextcloud and Firefly — they'd otherwise sit in a "pending" state while those containers stop, with a real (if narrow) chance of escalating into a false alert (see callout below).
  4. docker compose down all three stacks — Nextcloud, Firefly III, monitoring — in that order.
  5. Confirm nothing's left in docker ps, then hand off: the actual sudo shutdown has to be run by hand in its own terminal, since sudo has no controlling TTY through an automated session here (same quirk noted under Firefly III, above).
/post-restart-sequenceafter the power comes back▸
  1. Check both external mounts remounted before starting any container — starting Nextcloud against an empty mountpoint would look like a clean boot while quietly serving no data.
  2. docker compose up -d all three stacks — they were downed, not just stopped, so nothing restarts on its own.
  3. Health-check each service the same way the update runbooks do: occ status, the Firefly /health endpoint, container status for the rest.
  4. Resume the same 7 Kuma monitors once Nextcloud and Firefly are confirmed healthy — they stay paused across the reboot otherwise, since Kuma's data survives on a bind mount.
  5. After a RAM upgrade specifically, free -h to confirm the new total actually shows up.
Why the mounts don't care about USB re-enumeration

Both the RAID enclosure and the backup drive are mounted by filesystem UUID in /etc/fstab, not by device letter — so it doesn't matter if the kernel hands the RAID enclosure /dev/sdb instead of /dev/sda after a reboot, which USB devices do fairly often depending on enumeration order at boot. Both also carry nofail, so a drive that didn't reconnect cleanly won't hang the boot — which cuts both ways: it also means a missing mount won't announce itself as an error, so the post-restart check has to look for it explicitly rather than trusting a clean boot.

Debugging story — pgrep catching its own tail

The pre-shutdown check for a live backup process first ran as pgrep -fa restic, which matched every single time — including with no backup running. -f matches the full command line, and the check's own invocation was a shell one-liner that contained the word "restic" in its own text, so the process was matching itself. Switched to pgrep -x restic, which matches only the exact process name, not the full command line an unrelated process happens to mention.

Debugging story — the monitor that watched its own shutdown, then couldn't tell anyone

Expected Uptime Kuma to light up the phone during the first real shutdown; it didn't, just a few yellow squares. Turned out every monitor needs 5 consecutive failed checks a minute apart — about 5 minutes — before Kuma calls something actually down and fires a notification. The Nextcloud and Firefly containers each failed exactly once before Kuma's own container went down seconds later as part of the same monitoring stack shutdown, so nothing ever crossed the threshold. A monitor can't escalate an alert about an outage it's no longer running to witness. Beszel turned out to have real alert rules too (a correction to what this page used to say about it), with an even longer 10-minute grace period on host-down — but nothing it watches goes down before its own hub does, so it was never at risk the way Kuma was. Fix: both runbooks above now explicitly pause and resume just the 7 monitors that watch services stopped individually, ahead of the stack that watches them.

11

The actual operating system: Claude

Everything above reads like the work of a system administrator. It wasn't, not quite — it's the work of someone with modest sysadmin chops and an AI collaborator doing most of the actual administering, session after session, for months on end. Ubuntu is what the box boots into. Claude is what it's actually run through: every container recreated, every monitor wired up, every 3am backup script, every single "Debugging story" callout scattered through this page — diagnosed and executed by asking, not by having memorized a decade of Linux incantations first. Fair's fair; the credits roll for a reason.

Repeated processes become skills, not scripts

Every /update-* command and both shutdown runbooks above started life as a one-off conversation — "how do I update ntfy without breaking push notifications" — solved once, then promoted into a named, repeatable, self-verifying procedure instead of getting re-derived from scratch (or re-Googled, cap in hand) the next time it came up. That's the actual difference between a script, which someone has to remember exists and where it's buried, and a skill, which just gets invoked by name the moment it's needed and quietly checks its own work afterward.

Lessons become memories, not regrets

pgrep matching its own command line. Uptime Kuma alerting on an outage it stopped watching mid-collapse. A registry endpoint confidently lying about a container's real digest. The HEIC rabbit hole. Every one of those got learned exactly once, on this page and off it — none of them needed re-learning the hard way a second time, because the lesson stuck around the way a colleague's institutional memory does, not the way a terminal scrollback that gets cleared on reboot does.

None of this makes the hardware any less real, or the $234.65 line item above any less honest — but crediting the box alone would be like crediting the car for the road trip. What actually turned a dead 2020 desktop and a drawer full of spare parts into a monitored, backed-up, self-updating home lab wasn't someone who already knew how to do all this. It was someone who didn't, paired with an AI that did the parts they couldn't, remembered the parts they'd forget by next Tuesday, and turned "I had to figure this out once" into "this just handles itself now."
